`studio tui` is a keyboard client of the Studio that is already running on this machine. It is
laid out as the browser is: a sidebar with your teammates, their threads and your computers,
and a thread beside its computer, with the computer's tabs. Its Home is one list of what waits
for you, what works and what is quiet, and each request is answered with a key from its row. It
reviews git pushes, works in a thread with a teammate, types into any of a computer's
terminals, creates, forks and suspends computers, saves and restores their checkpoints, and
follows the network Activity. Everything else stays in the browser, and the TUI prints the
address of the page where you need it.

<TerminalScreen ansi={home} label="The TUI's Home: four requests waiting, a push, a group of hosts, an agent's permission and a host, then the quiet threads, a scheduled run and your computer, with the permission under the cursor read in full" />

## Start it

Start Studio as usual, then, in another terminal on the same machine:

```sh
studio tui
```

If Studio isn't running, the TUI says so and shows the command that starts it. It needs an
interactive terminal, and it signs in as you without asking for anything: only your user can
read the data that makes this possible, so run it on the machine Studio runs on. Over SSH, run
it there.

## The screen

The top row says where you are, as the browser's breadcrumb does: `Home`, `Iris`, `Iris › Fix
the login form`, or a computer's name. On its right are the computer on show, such as `● blub
▾` (`▾` when the teammate has more than one), and how many things wait for you.

What fits next to what depends on the width of your terminal:

- **Below 100 columns**, one thing at a time. `\` slides [the sidebar](#the-sidebar) in over
  the left, and `\` or `esc` closes it again. In a thread, `tab` shows its computer instead,
  and back.
- **From 100 columns**, the sidebar is always on the left.
- **From 120 columns**, a thread or a teammate's home has its computer beside it. At 140
  columns that's 30 columns of sidebar, 45 of thread and 63 of computer.
- **`alt+z`** gives the whole screen to what has the keys, and back.

Who has your keys is always in view:

- **A terminal**: its tab and the bottom line turn into a heavy line in the accent color. Every
  key goes to the terminal, `q` and `ctrl+c` too, except `ctrl+]`, which gives the keys back,
  `alt+1` to `alt+9`, which switch to another of the computer's terminals, `alt+z`, and `alt+a`,
  which [answers a request](#answer-from-a-terminal).
- **The composer** of a thread: `enter` sends, `ctrl+j` starts a new line, `esc` puts the draft
  aside. `/` at its start lists [commands](#commands-and-files), and `@` the computer's files.
- **Studio**: the sidebar, the thread or the computer beside it, marked by a bright line on its
  left. `\` moves to the sidebar and back, and `tab` between a thread and its computer.

`esc` goes back to the place you were before, and last to Home. `q` does too, except on Home
and in the sidebar, where it quits.

## Keys

The keys are the same everywhere, and they can't be changed. The key bar at the bottom shows
the ones that apply where you are, and `?` lists them all. `n` never means new: it says no to
a request, and `+` makes something new. `ctrl+k` [searches Studio](#search-studio) from
everywhere, the composer too, except while a terminal has the keys.

| `?` | help |
| `q` | quit |

When the TUI asks before it deletes a computer, restores a checkpoint or pushes, its choices are
in the line at the bottom, `y Delete  [n Keep]`. Keep is chosen, so `enter` keeps; press `y`, or
`←` and then `enter`, to go ahead. The terminal rings its bell once when a new request arrives.

## Home

Home lists everything in three sections:

- **Waiting for you**: every request of every environment, newest first: network requests,
  pushes to review, agents' permissions, memory and browser requests. A row says who asked,
  what for, where, and how long ago.
- **Working**: the threads at work.
- **Quiet**: [Lead's day](../lead/) once a teammate has worked today (`w` writes it now), the
  threads that ended in the last day, each teammate's next scheduled run, and
  your own computers.

Under them, **Earlier today** lists what you decided today.

The cursor starts on the top row, so the first request to arrive is under it, and after you
answer one it is on the next. `tab` goes to the next request. The keys on the row answer it,
as [its card](#requests) does: `y` allows, `n` denies, and on an agent's permission `a` allows
always. The key bar shows them. A push asks once more when you press `y`, because you haven't
read its whole diff: `y` again pushes it, any other key keeps it. `enter` opens the row: a
push's review, a permission's thread, a computer. A network decision that made a rule can be
taken back with `u` for a minute afterwards.

The reading pane shows the row under the cursor in full: a request's card with its choices, a
push's commits and diff, a thread's transcript or a computer's dashboard. It is under the list
in a narrow terminal and beside it in a wide one; `pgup` and `pgdn` scroll it.

## The sidebar

The sidebar has the sections of the browser's sidebar. It starts with **Home**, which counts
what waits for you. Then come:

- **Teammates**, each with its role and a count of what waits on it, with its threads under it.
  **+ New teammate** prints the address of the teammates page: making a teammate happens in the
  browser.
- **Your computers**, the ones without a teammate, and **+ New computer**.
- **Environment**: **Computers**, every computer of the environment, your teammates' too;
  **Network**, the network log, with the requests waiting; **Secrets** and **Memory**, which
  print their page's address; **Activity**; and **Settings**, with Studio's version.

Under each teammate are its threads, as in the browser: its agents running in a terminal, and
its threads that run, wait for you or continue in a terminal, plus the two latest others of
the last day. `+ n older` (or `→` on the teammate) shows the rest, and `←` hides them again. A
thread a teammate started on someone else's computer is listed under the teammate too. In a
narrow terminal, the sidebar slides in like this:

Each thread shows what it's doing:

- `●` working: a thread's turn runs, or the agent works in its terminal;
- `▲` waiting: it waits for you. For a thread, that's a permission, or another request of its
  computer while it is the teammate's latest thread there; for an agent in a terminal, a
  request on its computer;
- `✓` done: it finished since you last looked. It stays marked until you go in;
- `○` idle or ended, `◷` a scheduled task's run or a check-in, and `·` when the computer
  doesn't say (it started before this version of Studio).

**Computers** lists every computer with its owner, template, requests and status. `enter`
opens the one under the cursor, `t` its terminal, and `s` `z` `f` `h` `d` work on it as on its
Details tab. From 100 columns its dashboard is beside the list.

## Search Studio

`ctrl+k` finds anything in Studio by name, as `⌘K` does in the browser: a teammate, a
computer or one of its tabs, a thread, a place of the environment, a section of Settings, or
an action such as a new thread with a teammate, a new computer, or stopping the computer on
show. `/` opens it too, except in the composer and on Activity, where `/` filters by computer.

<TerminalScreen ansi={search} label="Search Studio over a thread: log finds the thread Fix the login form, Activity and General settings, grouped by kind with where each lives on the right" />

Type a few letters of a word: they match at a word's start, inside it, in order, or with one
typo, so `netwrok` finds Network and `log` finds Activity. A tab needs a word of its own:
`blub` finds the computer blub, `blub files` its Files tab. With nothing typed, the box lists
what you opened from it last and the first requests that wait for you; `enter` on a request
goes Home with the cursor on it.

| Key | In Search Studio |
|---|---|
| letters | search |
| `↑` `↓`, `ctrl+p` `ctrl+n`, `tab` | move |
| `enter` | open the hit |
| `>` first | search the actions only |
| `@` first | search the teammates only |
| `/` first | search the files of the computer on show; `enter` opens the file in its Files tab |
| `esc` or `ctrl+k` | close |

A click opens a hit, and a click outside the box closes it.

## A teammate's home

A teammate's row opens its home, as its page in the browser does: its role and how it's doing,
its computers (`◂` marks the one shown beside it), its open tasks, what it knows and its network
level. Under them is a box, `› Write to Iris…`: `enter` opens it, and your first prompt starts a
thread on the computer shown. `+` gives the teammate another computer, `t` opens its computer's
terminal, `c` its changes, and `m` shows its next computer.

## Thread

A thread opens from its row in the sidebar, from a request on Home, or with `+`. From 120
columns its computer is beside it; in a narrower terminal `tab` shows the computer instead.

<TerminalScreen ansi={thread} label="Iris's thread Fix the login form: the agent's tool calls folded to a line each, its answer with a code block, a network request it waits on as a card, and the box to write to Iris" />

The transcript streams as the agent works. Thoughts and tool calls are folded to one line:
`↑` and `↓` move between them, and `enter` expands one to show its files, its diff or its
output. `p` writes a prompt in the box at the bottom, framed in the thread's color: `enter`
sends it, `ctrl+j` starts a new line, and `esc` puts it aside. `x` stops the turn that runs.
Headings are bold, code is set off with a rule, and lists and tables are drawn as text.

When the agent asks for a permission or asks a question, you answer it in the thread. The
choices are listed under it.

- A permission: `y` allows it once, `a` always, `n` rejects it; the numbers pick one of the
  agent's options, and `i` ignores it. `←`, `→` and `space` work too.
- A question: press the number of a choice, or `r` to type an answer and `enter` to send it.
  `i` skips the question. A question with several fields is answered in the browser; its keys
  print the thread's address.

Any other request the thread waits on, such as a host one of the agent's tools wants or the push
it made, is a card above the box, and the card's keys answer it. `y` on a push asks once more,
and `enter` opens its review in the computer's Changes tab. `c` shows that tab too.

A thread that is an agent in a terminal, such as `claude`, is that terminal, with the
computer's Details beside it.

### Commands and files

Type `/` at the start of a prompt to list what you can do from the thread, and `@` to mention
a file of its computer. A box over the composer lists them, and what you type next filters it.

<TerminalScreen ansi={composerSlash} label="The composer of Iris's thread with / typed: a box over it lists /term, /files, /changes, /browser, /details, /threads, /new, /stop-computer and /home, each with what it does on blub" />

| Key | In the box |
|---|---|
| `↑` `↓` | move |
| `enter` | run the command, or add the file |
| `tab` | complete the command's name, or open the folder |
| `esc` | close the box and keep the text |

The commands are `/term`, `/files`, `/changes`, `/browser` and `/details` for the computer's
tabs, `/threads` for the teammate's threads in the sidebar, `/new` for a new thread, `/stop` to
stop the turn, `/start` and `/stop-computer` for the computer, and `/home`, `/search` and
`/keys`. Only the ones that can run are listed. A prompt that starts with `/` but names no
command is sent as it is.

`@` lists the computer's files, changed files first. Picking one writes `@web/src/login.tsx`
into the prompt, and the agent gets the file with it. Add `:12` or `:12-20` to name lines.

## The computer

A computer has the browser's tabs: **Terminal**, **Files**, **Changes**, **Browser** and
**Details**, and `1` to `5` pick them in that order. Only a teammate's computer has a browser,
so a computer of yours has no Browser tab.

### Terminal

The tab shows the computer's terminals as a row, `main  claude ▲  2  + new shell`, and under it
the one you picked, live. `enter`, or a click on it, gives it your keys, and `ctrl+]` gives them
back. `alt+1` to `alt+9` switch to another of the computer's terminals, `+` opens a new shell,
and `T` starts the teammate's agent in a new terminal.

Resizing your window resizes the computer's terminal. A terminal open in the browser too takes
the size of whichever window was resized last. Alt keys need a terminal that sends them; on
macOS, set Option to act as Meta.

### Files

The Files tab lists a folder of the computer, folders first, each file with its size and how
long ago it changed. Open a file and it shows beside the list with its line numbers, or as a
picture if it is one and your terminal has 256 colors or more. In a narrow pane, such as beside a
thread, the file takes the list's place.

<TerminalScreen ansi={files} label="The Files tab of Finn's computer api-refactor: the folder /workspace/src with components, login.tsx and main.tsx, and login.tsx open beside it with its line numbers" />

`j` and `k` move through the list. `enter` (or `→`) opens a folder or a file, and `←` (or
`backspace`) goes back up. `↑` and `↓` scroll the file, as do `pgup`/`pgdn` and `g`/`G`. `r`
switches between `/workspace`, `/tmp` and the whole computer. Files are edited and uploaded in
the browser: `o` prints the tab's address there.

The tab reads the folder again every 10 seconds, and a file when it changes. It shows files of
up to 1 MiB, and doesn't follow links.

### Changes

The Changes tab shows what git sees on the computer. On the left is a list in sections: the
pushes and pull requests that wait for you, the ones that went out in the last day with their
pull request's number, each repository under `/workspace` with its branch and its changes
(staged, not staged and untracked), and, beside a thread, the requests of the thread that
changed files. Beside the list is the row you're on: a push's review, a change's diff, or what
a request changed.

<TerminalScreen ansi={changes} label="The Changes tab of Finn's computer api-refactor: its repositories web and api, web's staged rename, two changes not staged and an untracked file, with the diff of src/login.tsx beside them" />

`j` and `k` move through the list, and the diff follows. `↑` and `↓` scroll it, `pgup`/`pgdn`
(or `b`/`space`) a page, `g` and `G` jump to its start or end, and `[` and `]` to the previous or
next file. `enter` on a repository folds its changes. In a narrow pane the list is one line over
the diff, `‹ M src/login.tsx +1 −1 · 3 of 7 ›`, and `j` and `k` still move through it.

The tab reads git every few seconds while it shows, and at once when a request of the thread
ends. A stopped computer's changes are read once it runs again.

#### Comments on lines

`c` puts a cursor on a line of the diff. Move it with `↑` and `↓`, press `enter` and write the
comment, and press `esc` when you're done. Where the comment goes depends on what you're
looking at:

- **A push that waits:** the comments stay on the push, each one under its line, until you
  decide it. `r` sends them along with what to change, and `L` leaves them on the push without
  deciding it, for the agent to read. `enter` on a line with a comment edits it; clear the text
  to take it back.
- **A change or a request:** the comment goes into the thread's box as
  `@web/src/login.tsx:13 Is this the only button?`, with the file attached for the agent as `@`
  does in the browser. Add more, or press `enter` to send them.

<TerminalScreen ansi={lineComments} label="A push to fix-login waiting in the Changes tab: a comment under its removed line, Why did this go? The test clicks the div, and the notice saying r sends it with what to change and L leaves it for Finn" />

### Browser

The Browser tab shows the page the teammate's browser is on: its address, who drove it last,
and the page itself if your terminal has 256 colors or more. It says when a rule blocked the
page or the page asks for a sign-in, and when the browser is asleep. You drive it, or wake it,
in the browser; the tab prints the address.

### Details

<TerminalScreen ansi={computer} label="The Details tab of Finn's computer api-refactor: its teammate, template, resources and uptime, CPU and memory, a request waiting, its history and its two open ports with their preview addresses" />

Details says what the computer is and does: its teammate, template, resources and uptime, its
requests and its open ports. On a taller terminal it is a dashboard: CPU over the last two
minutes as a chart, memory as a gauge with its history, the request waiting, the computer's
history, its network as it happens, and its teammate's browser.

Its keys work on the computer: `s` starts, stops or resumes it, `z` suspends it, `f` forks it,
`h` opens its checkpoints, `d` deletes it after asking, and `l` shows its Activity.

Each open port shows its preview address. In a terminal that shows links you can open it from
there. A preview needs a login the first time: open it once from the Ports section of the
computer's page in the browser, and after that the address from the TUI opens in that browser
for eight hours.

## Answer from a terminal

When a request waits on a computer, its Terminal tab shows a box over the terminal's top rows
that says what waits, such as `api-refactor wants proxy.golang.org:443`, with its choices as
buttons: **Allow once**, **Allow**, **Deny** and **More…** for a network request, **Review it**
for anything else.

- While Studio has the keys, press `o`, `y` or `n`, or move over the buttons with `←` and `→`
  and press one with `space`.
- While you type in the terminal, your keys go to the agent, so the box says `alt+a answers it`.
  `alt+a` puts you on its buttons; once you've answered, or pressed `esc`, your keys go back to
  the agent.
- `a` (**More…**) takes you to the request on Home, where you choose the host pattern and the
  scope. Once you've decided, you're back in the terminal.

## New computer

`+` on Home, or **+ New computer**, asks whose computer it is (a teammate, or none), what it
starts from (the base image or one of the environment's templates), and its name, which is also
its hostname: lowercase letters, digits and dashes. `enter` goes on and `esc` goes back a step.
Studio then creates the computer, and the TUI shows the steps as they happen, the image pull
with its progress, as the browser does. Once the computer is ready it opens. If the create
fails, you see the step and the reason, and `d` deletes the computer so you can try again. `esc`
leaves the create to finish on its own.

## Fork, suspend and checkpoints

These keys work on a computer's Details tab and on a row of the Computers list.

`f` forks the computer: a new computer with a copy of its disks. The TUI asks for the name at the
bottom, and suggests one that ends in `-fork`. `enter` forks, `esc` cancels. The fork's
progress then shows as a new computer's does.

`z` suspends a running computer. `s` resumes it.

`h` lists the computer's checkpoints, newest first. Each shows its name, whether the schedule
took it (auto) or you did (manual), and its age. A checkpoint holds the disks, not the running
programs.

- `n` saves a checkpoint. Type a name, or press `enter` alone to name it after the time. The
  computer must be running or stopped.
- `r` restores the checkpoint under the cursor. The TUI asks first, and `y` restores. Stop the
  computer first. What changed since the checkpoint is lost.

Deleting and renaming checkpoints, and their schedule, are in the browser.

## Requests

Each request is a row on Home, and its card is in the reading pane. Requests from one computer
that come in close together are one row and one card, as they are in the browser. A card lets
you:

- **Allow or deny** a network request. `y` and `n` make a rule for the host pattern and the
  scope you chose; `o` and `O` answer only this connection, with no rule; `1` allows it for
  an hour; `i` ignores it. When the browser opens a new site, `w` allows the site for the
  rest of that browser session, with no rule.
- **Choose what the rule covers:** `p` changes the host pattern (for a browser request, also
  its method and path), `s` the scope (the environment, your teammate's computers, or only this
  computer), and `x` any port instead of the default ports. For a browser request, `s` picks
  who the rule covers: its browser, all browsers, or the browsers and the computers.
- **Decide a group:** `A` allows every host, `D` denies every host, and `r` reviews them one by
  one.
- **Move with the arrows:** `←` and `→` move over the card's toggles (pattern, scope, ports)
  and its choices, and `space` or `enter` presses the one you're on, as its key does.
- **Review a git push:** `enter` opens the push in its computer's [Changes tab](#changes):
  the repository, branch and change, the commits, and the diff with added lines in green and
  removed ones in red. Scroll with the arrows, `pgup`/`pgdn` (or `b`/`space`), `g` and `G`.
  Then `y` approves and pushes it, `n` rejects it, `r` asks for changes in a note, with your
  [comments on its lines](#comments-on-lines), and `i` ignores it. From its row on Home, `y`
  asks once more before it pushes. A push that rewrites its branch lists the commits it drops,
  and only `F` force-pushes it. A pull request shows its title and description, and `y` opens
  it. Once it's out, the push is listed under PUSHED in the tab for a day, and in Activity under
  git. A push of a computer in another environment opens on its own, with the same keys.

The other cards are decided with their own keys:

- **Browser action:** `y` allows the click or the typing, `n` denies it, `i` ignores it. `r`
  makes the answer hold for every such action on that site.
- **Browser credential:** `y` fills the secret, `n` denies it, `i` ignores it. The agent sees
  only the secret's name.
- **Memory share:** `y` shares the fact with every teammate, `n` declines, `i` ignores it.
- **Memory conflict:** `1` keeps fact A and `2` keeps fact B; the other one is retracted. `i`
  leaves it for later. To keep both or edit one, `enter` prints the address of the memory page.
- **Agent permission:** `y` allows it once, `a` always and `n` rejects it, or press the number
  of one of the agent's options. `i` ignores it.

## Activity

`l` shows the log of the environment, newest first: each connection a computer made, with its
verdict, and each decision, with how it was made. `←` and `→` narrow it to one kind: network,
git, agents or memory. On a computer's Details tab, `l` shows only that computer; elsewhere `/`
filters by computer name. `enter` opens an entry with the rule that decided it: its host
pattern, action, ports, which computers it covers, where it came from, its hits and when it
expires. Without a rule, it says what decided instead, such as the curated list or a once
decision. The log refreshes every two seconds. Rules are edited in the browser; `r` prints the
address.

## Look and the mouse

On a terminal with colors, the TUI uses the browser's colors. Each area has its own hue:
network, git, browser, memory and threads. Status dots are colored, and a request is a card.

Wider terminals use the extra room:

- Home's reading pane is beside the list instead of under it.
- From 160 columns of the computer's pane, a diff is side by side, and `v` toggles it. `alt+z`
  gives the pane the whole terminal.
- The words a changed line changes are highlighted.
- What an action did, or an error, shows for 3 seconds at the top right.

The mouse works too:

- A click selects a row, and a double click opens it.
- A click on a column gives it the cursor, and a click on a terminal gives it your keys.
- A click on `≡` opens the sidebar, on the computer's chip shows the teammate's next computer,
  and on what waits goes Home.
- A click on a key, on a card or in the key bar, presses it, and a click on a row of the
  composer's box picks it.
- The wheel scrolls.
- Hold shift to select text as usual.

`STUDIO_TUI=plain studio tui` gives the plain look shown above, with no mouse and no charts. So
do `NO_COLOR` and terminals without colors. Every screen still fits 80×24.

## studio shell

From a shell, `studio shell <sandbox> [terminal]`, with the computer's name, opens one of its
terminals without the TUI around it: the computer's screen takes over your terminal, and your keys go to it.
`Ctrl-]` leaves. When a request arrives while you're in it, the terminal's title says what waits
and the bell rings; `Ctrl-]` then asks about the request in place, and once you've answered it
you're back in the terminal.

## Stays in the browser

Editing Settings, providers, teammates, templates, the rules, memory, secrets and the git forges
happens in the browser; `,` lists the sections with what each is set to, and `enter` prints the
address of one. Editing a computer's files, driving its browser and its DevTools, comments on a
range of a diff's lines, and a few answers stay there too: keeping both facts of a memory conflict,
questions with several fields, and a preview's first login. The TUI prints their addresses where
it has a key for them, and `?` shows where the browser is.
